# What makes platformer music catchy

This note collects research for WHITE SIGNAL's NES-style score (pulse 1, pulse 2, triangle, noise). It was written on 26 September 2026.

## Summary

1. Catchy game tunes repeat a short, singable hook many times, with just enough change to stay fresh.
2. The hook has a familiar up-then-down shape plus one odd leap or rhythm.
3. The best composers set tempo and rhythm from how the character moves, not from how the level looks.
4. One main theme, reused in the title, hub, levels, jingles and ending, makes a soundtrack feel like one game.
5. Lonely music stays catchy when the hook and groove stay and only the arrangement thins.

## The rules

Tests marked "suggested" are my proposals, not numbers from a study.

**R1. Hook early, hook often.** Put a 1 to 2 bar hook in the first 2 bars and bring it back at least 3 times per loop.
Why: in the "Hooked" recognition study, the fastest-recognised sections were the most repetitive and typical. Super Mario Bros also restarts its music on every life, so opening bars get heard most.
Shown by: Super Mario Bros overworld.
Test: the hook starts by bar 2 and appears 3 or more times per loop. A copy at another pitch counts.

**R2. Say it twice, then answer.** Pair phrases. The second copies the first one's rhythm, but the first ends open (on scale step 2 or 5) and the second closes (on 1). Arrange sections as A A B A or A B A.
Why: the repeat teaches the tune and the new ending stops it feeling stuck.
Shown by: Super Mario Bros 2 overworld, a textbook "sentence" (idea, repeat, continuation) in Alan Elkins' analysis.
Test: in each 8 bar section, bars 3 to 4 reuse at least three quarters of the rhythm of bars 1 to 2, and the two phrases end on different scale steps.

**R3. Familiar shape, one surprise.** Shape the hook as an arch, mostly steps and repeated notes, with one or two signature leaps of a fifth or more.
Why: Kelly Jakubowski's team compared earworms named by 3,000 people with similar songs. Earworms had common shapes, usually arches, plus unusual leaps. Tunes with an odd shape needed a very odd one to stick. Earworms were also slightly faster.
Shown by: Celeste's melody, which Lena Raine says draws a mountain on the page. DuckTales' Moon theme, which opens with an odd bar of 15 eighth notes.
Test: at least 60% of hook moves are steps (2 semitones or less) or repeats, 1 or 2 moves are 5 semitones or more, and the top note sits in the middle half.

**R4. Keep it singable.** Keep the hook within an octave to a tenth.
Why: two early studies linked earworms to easy singing, though Jakubowski's larger study did not repeat that result. Masato Nakamura wanted a melody players could hum during Sonic.
Shown by: Green Hill Zone, whose verse fits in a tenth.
Test: the hook spans 16 semitones or less.

**R5. Make the rhythm the fingerprint, and leave space.** Give the hook a tappable rhythm with at least one syncopation (a note that starts off the beat and holds across it). Prefer short, punchy notes and let rests frame the hook.
Why: Kondo built the Mario theme around a hi-hat push and favours short notes that punctuate a groove. He used silence to make the underground theme sparse and unpredictable.
Shown by: Super Mario Bros overworld and underground. Dr. Wily Stage 1 (Mega Man 2).
Test: at least one syncopated note, and a rest or pickup of half a beat or more before the hook. Suggested: a listener can name the track from the tapped hook rhythm.

**R6. Lead on top, pulse 2 fills the chords.** Make the lead the loudest and usually highest voice. Under its held notes, pulse 2 plays fast broken chords or a quieter, delayed echo.
Why: in the "Hooked" study, a prominent lead line was the strongest sound feature. Manami Matsumae (Mega Man) describes fast do-mi-so figures as the way three voices suggest full chords.
Shown by: Mega Man.
Test: the lead sits 2 to 4 volume steps (out of 15) above pulse 2 and is the highest note 90% of the time. Broken chords use only chord tones, at sixteenths or faster.

**R7. Answer the lead.** When the lead holds or rests, another voice moves.
Why: Kondo credits the audible second line (the obbligato) with keeping his Zelda tunes fresh. Takashi Tateishi built Dr. Wily Stage 1 by layering several melodies.
Shown by: Zelda's main themes. Dr. Wily Stage 1.
Test: every lead note or rest of 1 beat or more has another voice moving under it.

**R8. Make the bass a hook too.** Give the triangle its own rhythm (octave jumps, walking lines, a riff) instead of held root notes.
Why: a shaped bass gives the ear a second thing to remember.
Shown by: the Super Mario Bros 3 World 3 map, whose bassline was later reused for Mario Kart beach courses.
Test: in action tracks the bass moves at least every beat. Suggested: 4 bars of triangle alone should identify the track.

**R9. Make the loop point pull back in.** End the loop on the chord that wants to go home (V), with the lead leading into bar 1.
Why: an unfinished ending creates pull that the restart satisfies. Kirk Hamilton notes that Kondo's Hurry Up cue uses unresolved seventh chords for this kind of pull.
Shown by: the WHITE SIGNAL title theme's join from intro to loop.
Test: the last chord is V, and the last lead note is within 2 semitones of bar 1's first note.

**R10. Set tempo and metre from movement.** Choose both from what the player does in the level.
Why: Kondo scrapped his first Mario theme, written to suit the sky and grass, because it "didn't match the rhythm of Mario's running and jumping", then rewrote it after playing the prototype. He wrote the 3/4 underwater waltz first because it was easy to imagine, but he never said it matched swimming.
Shown by: Super Mario Bros overworld (about 100 half-note beats a minute). Dr. Wily Stage 1, Strike the Earth (Shovel Knight) and Green Greens (Kirby), all about 180. DuckTales' Moon theme, sped up from its prototype.
Test (suggested): the Spark's full jump lasts close to a whole number of beats, within 10%. Action stages run 140 to 200 beats a minute. Floating or water stages use 3/4, 6/8 or a slower beat.

**R11. Give each world its own dials.** Set mode, tempo band, rhythm feel, lead tone (pulse width) and drum pattern for each world. Keep the theme and change the dials.
Why: Kondo split Super Mario Bros music by place, and mixed bars of 3 and 4 beats underground to feel uneasy. Modes carry mood. Dorian, a minor scale with one brighter note, drives Vampire Killer (Castlevania) and Strike the Earth. Saria's Song (Zelda) uses the airy Lydian mode.
Shown by: Super Mario Bros 3, which gives each world its own map theme while stages share music by type.
Test: no two worlds share more than two of the five dials.

**R12. Lonely and hopeful, still catchy.** Keep the hook's rhythm and shape and change the dressing. Use fewer voices, a slower tempo, a softer tone, a major, Dorian or Lydian mode, and hooks that climb.
Why: Raine slowed Celeste's music to give it room to breathe, made piano Madeline's core sound, and saved all-minor writing for her dark side. Tateishi wrote Bubble Man quiet for its water stage, but not so quiet that his director would reject it. Hollow Knight's brief asked for melancholy with minimal instruments.
Shown by: Undertale's "Home". Donkey Kong Country's calm, widely remixed Aquatic Ambience.
Test: each quiet track states its hook in the original rhythm and has a section of 2 voices or fewer. Plain minor is kept for bosses and danger.

**R13. Plan for fatigue, especially in the hub.** Keep the hub slow (80 to 110 beats a minute), steady and sparse. Give long stays longer loops or variants.
Why: Kondo loops his tracks and revises any that tire him. He split Ocarina of Time's field music into 8-bar blocks played in random order. In Szpunar's 2004 study, liking for closely heard music rose then fell with repeats, while background music kept gaining. Idle players in a hub listen closely.
Shown by: Ocarina of Time's Hyrule Field.
Test: Kondo's check is 10 minutes on loop while doing something else. Suggested: a hub loop of 40 s or more with 3 or more different 4-bar blocks.

**R14. One main theme across the game.** Quote it in the title, hub, first and final levels, ending and jingles. Give each world a motif that all its levels share.
Why: Kondo arranged the same themes differently across Super Mario World. Undertale's opening melody returns in the menu, "Home", the finale and the ending. Raine wrote Celeste's first track as a reusable symbol of the game.
Shown by: Super Mario World's Athletic music, a ragtime version of the overworld tune.
Test: in a table of tracks against motifs, the main theme appears in the title, hub, final level, ending and two jingles.

**R15. Build jingles from the theme.** Make each jingle a short version of the hook or its rhythm, in the home key. Level clear rises and settles, death falls and game over is gentle.
Why: Kondo wrote nearly all Mario stings in C, the main theme's key. VGMPF notes he wrote the game over music to encourage another try.
Shown by: Super Mario Bros, where level clear runs about 5 s, death 2 s, game over 3 s and 1-up 1 s.
Test: each jingle runs 5 s or less (a world clear may run longer), uses its world's key or the main key, and holds the hook's first 3 to 4 notes or its rhythm.

## What this means for WHITE SIGNAL

Before this note, no track stated its hook more than twice a pass, and most leads spread over two octaves or more. On 26 Sep 2026 every melody was rewritten against these rules. `tools/audio/music_check.py` measures each loop's lead line and now reports all 16 loops inside every limit: the hook comes back 3 or more times, at least half the bars reuse earlier material, there is some syncopation, most moves are steps, the lead spans an octave and a fifth or less and stays at or below E6, and no strong note clashes with the bass.

FIRST LIGHT's hook (scale steps 5 1 1 2 3 1) now has its own rhythm, short LONG short short LONG short. The title plays it at half speed, the village quotes it, World 1 uses it in every level, and The Gate turns it minor and then major. The gaps this note found were handled as follows.

- **A1. Jingles skip the theme.** Done. Level clear, extra life and game over are now built from the hook (R15).
- **A2. The village is close but not quoting.** Done. Its middle quotes the hook on G and then on A (R14).
- **A3. The Aerials motif is wide.** Done. It now ends a third up instead of a fifth (1 5 9 8 10), so it spans 16 semitones (R4).
- **A4. Test tempo against the jump.** Checked, not changed. A full jump lasts 0.70 s. That is about 2 beats in the fast levels and 1 beat in 3-1 and 2-2, but in between elsewhere. The evidence for matching exactly is weak, so the tempos stay (R10).
- **A5. Option: answer the call.** Still open for the ending (R14).
